To 600, the list is Christology and the Trinity: the prologue of John, “I and the Father are one,” the self-emptying of Philippians 2, the one mediator. The controversies of the fourth and fifth centuries are visible in the arithmetic.

601–1000 turns to judgment and the Christian life: the sheep and the goats of Matthew 25, “learn of me, for I am meek,” love of enemies.

1001–1216 reads like a rule of life: “one heart and one soul” (Acts 4:32, the opening of the Rule of Augustine that the canons regular lived by), “our citizenship is in heaven,” “I desire to be dissolved,” “God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross,” “blessed are the poor in spirit.”

Counts are distinct works, not occurrences, so one long commentary does not swamp the list. Migne’s volumes reprint older editions whose editors cited differently; only the volumes that cite to the verse contribute here. Matching the text against the Vulgate as well (below) finds every verse but 1,356 of 35,811 quoted somewhere. The unquoted cluster in Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees and Ezra–Nehemiah.

2 · The four senses

Where the senses of Scripture are stated

Henri de Lubac’s Exégèse médiévale argued that the Latin Middle Ages read Scripture under four senses: history, allegory, tropology and anagogy. Here the PL is searched for every place that names three or more of them together.

The search finds 269 such places. Two independent codings agree on what kind of passage each is (kappa 0.93), and 132 are statements of theory, not passing lists or applications.

The figure most often used to teach the senses is Jerusalem: a city of stone, the Church, the soul, the heavenly city, in 28 passages. Next is the four feet of the table in the tabernacle.

3 · Machine and reader

What a count finds, and what only a reader finds

De Lubac read the sources by hand. Setting the machine’s 132 statements against his footnotes shows what each can see.

  • He cites 96 of the 132 for exactly this point. For the 11 he does not cite at all, a reader still has to confirm the omission.
  • Of his own citations of passages naming three or more senses, the word search finds half. In 98 of the places it misses, relaxing a rule (a wider window, a broader word list) would find them.
  • In 64 places, the scheme is carried by a figure, not a word: the three ways of cooking a sacrifice, four casks in a cellar, shadow and image and truth, the three comings of Christ. No vocabulary will ever find these. They are what a reader contributes, and the reason the project keeps a reading layer beside the count.

4 · Which Bible?

The early Fathers quote a different Latin

Adding Old Latin texts to the index (Sabatier’s 1743 Versiones antiquae, and the 37 Old Latin manuscripts of John transcribed by the IGNTP) recovers about 4 more points of the early Fathers’ New Testament citations, and 7 on John. Most of the gap remains. Much of it is allusion, not quotation: the early Fathers wove Scripture into their own sentences. They cite differently, not only from a different text.

5 · Before the four senses

Origen in Greek, and what his translators made of him

The names of the senses come from Origen’s Greek: allēgoria, tropologia, anagōgē. The Latin West read most of Origen in translation. Did the translators carry his words over, or change them?

Migne’s volume 13 of the Patrologia Graeca answers on one page. For Origen’s Commentary on Matthew it prints three texts of the same passage: Origen’s Greek; a facing Latin by Migne’s early-modern editors; and below both, an ancient Latin translation, the vetus interpretatio. Over 293 such pages:

TermOrigen’s GreekEarly-modern LatinAncient Latin
tropologia21170
anagoge640
allegoria767
moralis, moraliter–026

The ancient translator never writes tropologia. Where Origen has it, he writes moraliter, “morally”: on 14 of the 18 pages that carry the Greek word. The early-modern editors keep tropologia (17 times of 21) and never write moraliter. Only allegoria passes into the ancient Latin as itself. The medieval equation of tropology with the moral sense has a visible step here, in a translator’s choice of word.

And Origen’s own Greek does not use the four-sense scheme. Across 373,000 words of it, he names three senses together twice, and both times allegory and anagogy are one reading named twice (“the allegories, by way of anagogy, of the histories recorded”). His habit is a pair: the letter set against one higher reading, which he calls allegory, anagogy or tropology as the passage suits. The fourfold grid looks like a Latin construction, built in part by translators.

Counts are per page, not word-for-word alignments. The Greek was read by a handwriting-recognition model tested against checked text (it reproduces 97.7–99.8% of words, and 262 of 269 sense words); the Latin by ordinary OCR. The date of the ancient Latin version is disputed (5th–6th century). Only Origen is read so far; the rest of the Patrologia Graeca is being read volume by volume.

Method

How this was made

Everything above was computed, not read by a language model: the Corpus Corporum transcription of the PL (5,276 works), the Clementine Vulgate, and a citation spine with three streams. The streams are Migne’s parenthetical citations, rare five-word sequences shared with the Vulgate, and ordered word pairs for looser wording. De Lubac’s footnotes were transcribed into a ledger. Only aggregate counts are published here. The Greek is Migne’s Patrologia Graeca, read from public-domain scans by a handwriting-recognition model (Kraken) running locally.

Liturgical and formulaic uses of Scripture (“in the name of the Father, and of the Son…”, the doxologies, the versicles of the office) are counted, not excluded. Scripture was prayed before it was studied, and the line between the two is a modern one. Each such use is tagged, so a reader can set it aside, or study it.

These are preliminary findings. The Greek Fathers are read only for Origen so far, and the 11 uncited passages await a reader. Numbers will move.
